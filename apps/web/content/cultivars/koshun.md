# Koshun

Koshun is a Shizuoka sencha cultivar bred for one trait above the rest: an orchid-like floral
aroma that its own release material describes as unmistakably different from Yabukita's, and
that difference was the entire point. Crossed in 1970 from two locally-bred aroma cultivars,
Kurasawa and Kanayamidori, and registered in 2000, it belongs to a run of late-twentieth-century
Shizuoka cultivars bred not to replace Yabukita at scale but to give individual growers something
distinctive to sell under their own name. It has become one of the more commercially interesting
aroma cultivars to come out of the prefecture, sold as single-cultivar sencha, pan-fired
kamairicha and, at specialist shops, a withering-style black tea.

## Lineage

- **Kurasawa** (くらさわ) ♀ — an unregistered 1967 selection made at the same Shizuoka station,
  chosen from naturally-pollinated Yabukita seedlings rather than a deliberate cross. Its trial
  designation was Shizu-7111, and it belongs to a loosely-grouped "7000 series" of Yabukita-derived
  Shizuoka selections from the same years, alongside Yamakai, Suruga-wase and Fuji-midori. It
  contributes a forest-like, woodland aroma and a fairly heavy astringency of its own, and — as
  matcha — a distinctive cocoa note.
- **Kanayamidori** (かなやみどり) ♂ — 茶農林30号, bred not at the prefectural station but at the
  national tea experiment station at Kanaya, and registered in 1970 from a 1949 cross of a zairai
  selection (S6) as seed parent with Yabukita as pollen parent. It contributes the other half of
  the aroma-forward pairing: a cultivar known for a sweet, milky fragrance closer to fresh dairy
  than to anything in the standard vocabulary of green tea.

This is a controlled cross rather than a landrace selection, but an unusual one in that neither
parent is itself a fully "clean" cultivar in the pedigree sense. Kurasawa was picked out of an
open-pollinated seedling population, so its own pollen parent is unrecorded; Kanayamidori is a
proper controlled cross, but one generation removed from Yabukita on its own account. The result
is that Koshun carries Yabukita in its background on both sides — through Kurasawa directly, and
through Kanayamidori's paternal line — even though it was selected for and is prized precisely
for smelling nothing like it. No cultivars bred using Koshun as a parent in turn have been
located in the course of this research.

## History

Kurasawa and Kanayamidori both belong to a documented wave of Shizuoka cultivar selection
spanning the mid-1960s to mid-1970s, driven by surging domestic demand and the growing scale of
tea-processing machinery, with breeding aimed at higher-yielding, differently-timed, higher-quality
cultivars. Koshun's own cross was made in 1970 at the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station,
using Kurasawa (itself only three years out of selection) as the seed parent and the
newly-registered Kanayamidori as the pollen parent.

The chronology from there is unusually well documented for a Shizuoka prefectural cultivar:

- **1970** — crossed: Kurasawa × Kanayamidori.
- **1978** — entered clonal (vegetative) comparison trials under the strain name 70-11-6.
- **1987** — entered the prefecture's recommended-cultivar determination trial.
- **1995** — breeding formally completed (育成).
- **1996** — application filed on 21 October for registration under the Seed and Seedling Law
  (種苗法) as application No. 9199, under the cultivar name Koshun; published 12 March 1999.
- **2000** — registered on 27 June as 品種登録第8131号.

That is a thirty-year span from cross to registration, and it is worth being precise about the
gap between 1996 and 2000: the earlier date is when the registration application was filed, not
when it was granted, and at least one secondary source conflates the two by describing 1996 as
the registration year. The station's own material and independent registry listings agree on
2000 as the year Koshun was actually registered.

Koshun was one of two flagship releases — alongside Tsuyuhikari — of a broader shift the
station's own retrospective frames explicitly: from the Showa-era priorities that had produced
Kurasawa and Kanayamidori (yield, timing, mechanisation) to a Heisei-era one, prompted by the
end of the bubble economy and a more diversified market, of breeding "cultivars distinguished by
flavour". The framing given for Koshun specifically sets out three intended shifts: from
undifferentiated cultivars toward branding, from shared co-operative processing factories toward
growers who cultivate, process and sell their own leaf, and from the mass market toward a niche
one.

### An aroma the industry wasn't yet built for

The same retrospective is candid about the problems Koshun posed during development, and they
are worth keeping alongside the flattering description of its aroma. Because it buds in the same
mid-season window as Yabukita, it did nothing to ease the scheduling pressure a shared processing
factory faces at peak harvest — unlike an early or late cultivar, it competes for the same
capacity rather than spreading it. Its buds are numerous but small and harden quickly, a habit
poorly suited to mechanised harvesting. It was judged short on umami and fairly astringent. And
above all, its aroma was strong and entirely unlike Yabukita's, at a time when Yabukita was
effectively the industry's quality standard and anything that smelled different from it was read
as a fault rather than a feature — the same reception Kanayamidori's milky aroma had met a
generation earlier. Koshun's eventual success rests on that expectation having shifted by the
time it reached the market.

A retrospective article on Koshun published in the trade magazine 月刊「茶」 (Shizuoka Prefecture
Tea Council) and reproduced by O-CHA NET is credited to 中村順行, a researcher associated with the
cultivar's development and later with a University of Shizuoka group that has continued to publish
on it.

## Characteristics

Koshun is plucked medium (中生), in the same window as Yabukita rather than distinctly earlier or
later — the registration filing rates its budding slightly early (ほう芽期やや早) and only its
plucking date mid-season — a deliberate trait of the cross, though one that, as above, was seen
as a limitation rather than an advantage when the cultivar was new. The tree is vigorous with an
open, spreading habit, which makes it easy to train while young; as a garden matures its bud-number tendency
strengthens, and growers are advised to plan renewal pruning to keep yield up. Reported yield is
high, particularly from young bushes, and higher than Yabukita's. Cold resistance is strong
enough that it is recommended across the whole of Shizuoka Prefecture, but it is susceptible to
Kuwashiro scale insect (white peach scale) and needs careful pest control; one vendor source adds
that it resists anthracnose slightly better than Yabukita but is weak against bacterial shoot
blight, though this has not been corroborated elsewhere. Processed leaf rolls finely and holds a
uniform shape.

The cup is the reason the cultivar exists, and the sources are consistent on its character even
where their vocabulary differs. Shinkoju, the single-cultivar specialist that stocks it, describes
its defining trait plainly as a "characteristic orchid-like aroma" (蘭のような特徴的な香り). The
breeding station's own sensory work is more specific still: panellists describing Koshun's
withering aroma (萎凋香 — the fragrance drawn out by a withering step in processing) reached for
rose-like sweet floral notes, milkiness and fruitiness, and reported that the aroma intensifies
with longer withering; the two aroma compounds singled out in that work are geraniol, associated
with fresh rose, and linalool oxide IV. Consumer preference panels rated Koshun highly for
aroma quality, taste intensity and umami, with younger women in particular describing the aroma
as fresh and cool. Retail tasting notes from Shinkoju's own product lines add shiso and cherry-leaf
notes in lightly-steamed sencha, apple-candy in one black tea grade, and honey with fruit in a
premium black tea. Set against this, the tea is only moderately umami-rich and can read as fairly
astringent, particularly in the finish — a trade the breeding programme itself acknowledged rather
than concealed.

Processing favours that aroma over body: deep steaming (fukamushi) is explicitly discouraged, since
it blunts the fragrance that is the cultivar's whole appeal, and growers are advised to extend
steaming only slightly, and only as far as needed to soften astringency, before the aroma starts
to suffer. Standard steaming produces a bright yellow liquor with a long finish. Koshun also takes
well to pan-firing: in a 2006 sensory panel comparing kamairicha made from Koshun and from
Yabukita, Koshun scored markedly higher for first-flush aroma. Its readiness to develop a strong
withering aroma has also made it a candidate for withering-based processing outside plain sencha,
and it is sold in that form — as a black tea with a honeyed, fruity character — by at least one
specialist retailer, alongside its more usual sencha and kamairicha. Recorded plantings are small:
one Shizuoka prefectural dataset puts Koshun at 12 ha against 139 ha for Tsuyuhikari, its
contemporary from the same breeding shift, which places it as a boutique rather than a mainstream
cultivar even within its own prefecture.
