# Asahi

Asahi is the cultivar Uji growers reach for when the target is competition-grade matcha. It was
selected in 1954 from a native (zairai) tea garden in Uji City by the farmer Hirano Jin'nojō, then
trialled from 1953 and named by the Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research Institute. It has never carried
a national Cha Norin number — it is a prefectural recommended cultivar, grown almost nowhere
outside Kyoto. Where Yabukita is the all-purpose workhorse behind three-quarters of Japan's tea
acreage, Asahi sits close to its opposite: a narrow-window, hand-picked specialist bred for one job
— tencha destined for high-grade and competition matcha — and one with a standing reputation among
growers as the hardest of Kyoto's shade cultivars to farm well.

## Lineage

- **Uji zairai (landrace) tea tree, Hirano's selection** — an individual bush picked out of a
  seed-grown Uji tea garden, not a controlled cross. Its old strain name, Hirano No. 11
  (平野11号), is the number the selecting farmer gave it in his own trial stock.

Because Asahi is a landrace selection rather than a bred cultivar, it has no recorded pollen
parent — the tree was chosen for how it already grew, not bred toward a target. Kyoto's tea trade
body groups it today with two other cultivars finalised in the same year, 1954: Samidori (selected
independently by the tea merchant Koyama Masajirō, old strain name Koyama No. 69) and Ujihikari
(bred directly by the institute itself, old strain name Kyōken No. 170). Asahi is the odd one out
among the three — Samidori came from a merchant's own numbered trial series, Ujihikari from the
institute's breeding programme, but Asahi was found and numbered by a working farmer in his own
field. No cultivars are recorded as having been bred from Asahi.

Uji's tea research institute lists a fourth tencha cultivar alongside these three, 展茗 (Tenmyo,
registered 2006), bred later specifically for machine harvesting; the institute's own note on
Tenmyo's quality — "ほぼ同等で優れる" ("comparable to and as good as") Samidori and Asahi — treats
Asahi's cup as the benchmark the newer, machine-pickable cultivar was measured against.

## History

### A trio finalised in 1954

Systematic selection of individual Uji zairai trees ran through the early-to-mid twentieth
century, with different growers evaluating candidates in their own fields before the Kyoto
Prefectural Tea Research Institute took the strongest of them in for formal trial. Asahi, Samidori
and Ujihikari all reached that end point in the same year, 1954, and the institute's own summary of
the group is unambiguous about what they were for: "京都府では、'あさひ、さみどり、うじひかり'が
主にてん茶用品種として栽培されています" — in Kyoto Prefecture, Asahi, Samidori and Ujihikari are
the cultivars mainly grown as tencha, all under intensive, hand-picked management, and together
they form the high-grade tencha that represents the Uji tea brand.

### Found, not bred

What sets Asahi apart within that trio is who found it. Ujihikari was developed inside the
institute; Samidori came from Koyama Masajirō, the head of a tea house running his own numbered
trial series. Asahi came from Hirano Jin'nojō, an individual farmer, working his own tea garden in
Uji City rather than an institutional programme. The institute's records describe him simply as
"平野（ひらの）甚之丞（じんのじょう）氏" — Mr Hirano Jin'nojō — with no title attached. The
institute took his selection in for trial from 1953 and formally named and adopted it the following
year. It was never carried onto a national registry: it holds no Cha Norin number and no Plant
Variety Protection Act registration, and its formal standing is, and has only ever been, as one of
Kyoto Prefecture's recommended cultivars (茶奨励品種).

### A competition standard

The institute's own one-line verdict on Asahi's quality is direct: 「てん茶として特に優秀、出品茶
用として多用される」 — "particularly excellent as tencha, widely used for exhibition [competition]
tea." That reputation has held for seventy years. Horii Shichimeien, a long-established Uji tencha
producer, describes Asahi in its own words as "the best variety of tencha found in Uji" and "a
high-quality tea that dominates the top ranks at tea competitions," and named a stone-ground matcha
after it — Mumon (無門) — replanting the cultivar in the first year of Reiwa (2019) for a first
picking targeted six years later, in Reiwa 6 (2024), which is itself a measure of how long a new
Asahi planting takes to become productive. Matcha Direct, a Uji tencha wholesaler, ranks Asahi
fifth among Kyoto Prefecture's tea cultivars by production volume — behind Yabukita, Okumidori,
Samidori and Gokou — describing it as "a rare cultivar of Uji Tea that is traded at high prices...
not widely distributed," and quotes a farmer's verdict that has the flavour of a proverb: "the more
effort you put into this cultivar, the more it rewards you."

## Characteristics

Asahi buds at medium timing (中生), on par with Yabukita — not early, despite that claim
circulating in some secondary write-ups (see `conflicts`). The tree grows upright with good early
vigour, and under shade its new leaf is vivid green, thin and notably large. That leaf shape is
exactly wrong for a rolled tea: Matcha Direct notes plainly that the leaves are "unsuitable for
rolled tea like Gyokuro," which is why Asahi is grown almost exclusively for tencha rather than for
gyokuro or sencha.

Kyoto Prefecture's own cultivation table is candid about where Asahi is hard work. Yield is rated
やや少, "somewhat low," among the lowest of the prefecture's recommended cultivars, and cutting
propagation is rated やや不良, "somewhat poor" — new plantings are harder to establish from
cuttings than Yabukita, Samidori or Ujihikari. Disease resistance is middling rather than strong:
medium tolerance of anthracnose and only "somewhat weak" resistance to gray blight (輪斑病). The
same table singles Asahi out with a caution not applied to most of its neighbours — its new buds
are sensitive to late-spring frost and need watching for it — a specific vulnerability distinct
from, and not necessarily contradicted by, the general "cold-hardy" reputation the cultivar
otherwise carries in enthusiast sources. Add to that a picking window that is short even by Uji
standards, and the crop is taken by hand rather than machine; the extra labour goes into quality
rather than volume.

What that labour buys is the cup Uji growers compete on: concentrated colour that takes shading
well ("染まりが良く"), a clear, strong aroma, and a mellow, rounded flavour with little bitterness,
described elsewhere as showing sustained umami and a body that "fills the palate in a way that
lighter cultivars cannot." Properly shaded and stone-ground, it is the tea that other Uji
cultivars — and at least one newer, easier-to-machine-pick cultivar bred specifically to match it —
are still measured against.
