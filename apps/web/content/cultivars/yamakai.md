# Yamakai

Yamakai is an open-pollinated seedling of Yabukita, trialled at the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea
Experiment Station from 1949 and released to growers in 1967 — yet it has never carried a Cha
Norin number or a Plant Variety Protection Act registration. What kept it out of the national
registry was not quality but character: its aroma is strong, food-like and genuinely divisive, the
kind of trait mid-century blenders had no use for in a cultivar meant to disappear into a uniform
sencha blend. Decades later that same trait is exactly why single-cultivar producers seek it out.
Shizuoka Prefecture never let it lapse either — it remains one of the prefecture's recommended
cultivars, and is sold today as sencha, fukamushi sencha, kabusecha and gyokuro, and, by a few
specialist producers, even as kamairicha and black tea.

## Lineage

- **Yabukita** (seed parent) — an open-pollinated seed taken from an existing Yabukita bush, not a
  controlled cross; the pollen parent was never recorded.

Yamakai is one of a small cohort of Yabukita-seedling selections carried through trials at the
Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station in the late 1960s under the internal breeding-line
number Shizu-7166, alongside Suruga-wase (Shizu-7109), Kurasawa (Shizu-7111) and Fuji-midori
(Shizu-7224) — plus a further line, Shizu-7132, which like Yamakai was never formally registered.
Because each of these lines began as an unplanned open-pollination event on an existing Yabukita
plant, none has a documented pollen parent, and none is recorded as having gone on to parent a
later registered cultivar in its own right.

## History

The seedling that became Yamakai was taken into trial at the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment
Station (now the Shizuoka Tea Research Center) in 1949. It was evaluated there for the better part
of two decades before being named Yamakai and having its cultivation actively promoted across the
prefecture in 1967 — the date this record uses as its public debut, though it corresponds to no
national registration.

That absence is not an oversight. Japan's first slate of nationally registered tea cultivars (茶農林,
Cha Norin) was set in 1953 and has been added to steadily since; a full listing running from
Cha Norin No. 1 through No. 52 (compiled in the reference table 附表1 茶農林登録品種一覧) contains no
entry for Yamakai, and it is likewise absent from a separately compiled registry table of both
Cha Norin and Plant Variety Protection Act cultivars. The Plant Variety Protection Act itself did
not exist until 1978, more than a decade after Yamakai was already being planted commercially in
Shizuoka; by the time tea varieties could be filed under it, Yamakai had long since been
distributed to growers, which would have told against the novelty a Plant Variety Protection Act
filing requires. So Yamakai holds neither registration — not because it was rejected, but because
the two registries it might have entered were either the wrong shape for it or came too late.

What it does hold is prefectural recognition. Yamakai has its own entry in the Shizuoka Tea
Industry Council's "notable tea cultivar" guidebook, drawn from the same 『茶』 journal that is one
of the better characteristics sources for Shizuoka cultivars generally, and it is named there among
the prefecture's recommended cultivars outside Yabukita. Commercial interest in it has never fully
died: the Kagoshima Tea Experiment Station included Yamakai as one of thirteen major green-tea
cultivars in a multi-year regional adaptability trial run at Mizobe, Aira district, from 1987, with
comparative growth, frost-damage and yield data published across several instalments of 茶業研究報告
(Chagyo Kenkyu Hokoku, the Tea Research Journal) between 1989 and 1991 — evidence that even without
a national number, breeders kept using it as a comparison point against Yabukita and other
registered cultivars.

Accounts differ slightly on why the name was chosen. One Japanese specialist reference explains it
as a reference to the new shoots and made tea both having a mountain-tea-like character; an
English-language reference instead attributes it to the liquor's colour resembling mountain-grown
tea. The two also disagree on the kanji used — 山峡 against 山狭 — though both are read やまかい and
both point at the same "mountain ravine" imagery.

## Characteristics

Yamakai buds at the same time as Yabukita, which puts it in the medium (中生) band rather than early
or late. Its cold resistance is rated medium, but that average conceals a specific weakness: in the
Kagoshima regional trial, Yamakai was one of the cultivars that suffered conspicuously more frost
damage than the group average, in company with Asahi and Uji-hikari, even though its own harvest
timing was not markedly later than Yabukita's. Disease resistance is mixed — weak against
anthracnose and bacterial shoot blight, medium against blister blight and gray blight, and somewhat
susceptible to the mulberry (white peach) scale insect. Mature leaves are deep green, slightly
small and thin, with a diamond shape. Yield is reported as roughly on par with Yabukita, and it
takes well to covered cultivation, which is a large part of why it has kept a following among
kabusecha producers.

Made as sencha, the leaf rolls tightly to a good green colour and brews a clear green liquor with
sweetness, umami and a refreshing finish; Japanese retailers note it performs particularly well in
second and third flush, sometimes even outdoing Yabukita there. But the trait everyone comes back
to is the aroma, and it is not a conventional green-tea aroma at all: English-language tasting notes
describe something food-like — sour notes, mayonnaise, oil — while Japanese vendors reach for
cured-ham-and-melon and roast-meat comparisons, and independent reviewers describe a gourmet
raspberry-jam or red-fruit character depending on how it is roasted. Accounts agree this is
polarising rather than universally pleasant, which is precisely the property that kept it out of
uniform blends for decades and is now the reason single-cultivar sellers list it at all. Beyond
straight sencha and fukamushi sencha, a few specialist producers also process it as kamairicha and
even as black tea, chasing the same aromatic intensity through different routes.
