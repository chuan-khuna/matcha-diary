# Asanoka

Asanoka is a sencha cultivar bred at the Kagoshima Prefectural Tea Experiment Station and registered
in 1996, crossed from Yabukita and a line recorded only as Cp1. It is grown as a regional specialty
rather than at national scale, valued chiefly for a distinctive varietal aroma, a finely rolled leaf
with good colour, and an unusually high amino acid content that has made it a subject of research into
low-nitrogen, organically-managed cultivation. Trade coverage treats it as one of the more interesting
cultivars to come out of Kagoshima in the 1990s without ever suggesting it displaced Yabukita there;
a 2019 reference work on tea cultivars still describes it as "locally notable" rather than mainstream.

## Lineage

- **Yabukita** female — the industry benchmark cultivar, contributing a reliable, balanced base onto
  which Asanoka's aroma is layered.
- **Cp1** male — a breeding line with no traceable public documentation of its own origin. See
  "The identity of Cp1" below for what little can be established about it.

This is a controlled cross, not a landrace selection: Asanoka was deliberately bred and then selected
from the resulting seedlings, though no source gives a separate crossing year distinct from the 1996
registration date. No cultivars are documented as descendants of Asanoka, and it does not appear to be
a parent in any other registered cultivar's pedigree found in this research.

## History

Kagoshima Prefectural Tea Experiment Station registered Asanoka in 1996. Notably, it does not carry a
茶農林 (Cha Norin) number: the national MAFF registry of Cha Norin-numbered cultivars runs from 茶農林41号
(Fushun, 1991) to 茶農林42号 (Minamisayaka, 1991) to 茶農林43号 (Hokumei, 1992) to 茶農林44号 (Benifuuki,
1993) and then jumps straight to 茶農林45号 (Ryofu, 1997) — no number was issued for 1994–1996. Asanoka
was registered by the prefecture under the Seed and Seedling Law (種苗法) — the Plant Variety Protection
Act framework — rather than through the national Cha Norin system, which several sources bear out by
listing it separately as a Seed-and-Seedling-Law-registered rather than MAFF-registered cultivar.

Trade press picked it up twice in the years after registration: a 1998 feature in the industry magazine
「茶」grouped it with Hokumei and Mie Midorimoe No. 1 as a notable cultivar bred by a major tea
prefecture, and a 2001 profile in the same magazine singled it out by name — "いま注目の品種を見る(23)
あさのか" ("Cultivars worth watching now, no. 23: Asanoka") — describing it as finely rolled, of
excellent colour, and high-yielding. More recently, a 2023 paper in the JATAFFジャーナル developed a
pruning technique specifically to reduce net blister blight (mochi disease) incidence in Asanoka grown
under organic management, which places it among the cultivars Kagoshima growers are using for
chemical-free production. None of this amounts to wide adoption: a 2019 reference book from the
Shizuoka Tea Industry Council, 茶の品種 新版, still files Asanoka under "ローカルながら注目したい品種"
— cultivars that are local rather than national in reach but worth paying attention to regardless.

### The identity of Cp1

No source found in this research states outright what Cp1 is or where it came from — not the breeding
station, not the trade press, not any registry table. Two pieces of circumstantial evidence point,
without confirming, toward Chinese-type (中国種) germplasm. First, a 1988 chloroplast-DNA study
published in 茶業研究報告 examined leaf material from six named tea lines — A6, Kan6, Ck17, Cp1,
Yabukita and Yamatomidori — alongside the wild relative *Camellia taliensis*, and discusses its result
in terms of differences among "アッサム雑種、中国種、日本種" (Assam hybrids, Chinese types and Japanese
types) found within the *C. sinensis* material tested; A6 is independently identified elsewhere as an
Assam hybrid, and Yabukita and Yamatomidori are unambiguously Japanese landrace-derived cultivars,
which leaves Cp1 (along with Kan6 and Ck17) as one of the lines standing in for the remaining
categories in that three-way comparison. Second, the same national registry table that documents the
Cha Norin series separately records 茶農林22号 Benifuji (1960, bred at Kanaya) as a cross of
Benihomare × C19 — a second "C"-prefixed line used as a breeding parent at the same national
institute lineage. Taken together this is consistent with a "C" prefix denoting Chinese-type
germplasm held at Japan's national tea breeding programme, which would also line up with Asanoka's
reputation for a "distinctive" aroma unlike Yabukita's own — but it falls short of a documented fact,
and it is presented here as an inference, not a sourced certainty. The alternative reading — that "C"
marks something else entirely, as it does in at least one other prefecture's own numbering, where a
"C" in Miyazaki's "MC3" line denotes Caucasus-type germplasm rather than Chinese — cannot be ruled out
for Kagoshima's own internal scheme.

## Characteristics

Asanoka buds mid-season (中生); no source gives a specific day-count relative to Yabukita, so its
position within the wider early/late spread this collection otherwise measures in days cannot be
stated precisely. Growth is vigorous, particularly in the second flush — summer growth is described as
strong — and yield is repeatedly reported as high, though always in qualitative terms ("多収") rather
than as a published kg/10a figure.

In the cup and in the dry leaf, Asanoka is known for three things: a distinctive varietal aroma unlike
Yabukita's grassy character, a balanced flavour with a mellow, mild finish, and — worked into leaf —
a fine, tightly rolled shape with good colour and lustre, praised specifically in a 1998–2001 trade
assessment. Underlying the mellow taste is an unusually high amino acid content: research into tea
cultivars suited to reduced-nitrogen fertilisation found that Asanoka retains notably higher amino
acid levels in its winter wood tissue than the other cultivars tested, and shows correspondingly high
nitrogen-utilisation ability above ground — the basis for its being positioned as a candidate for
environmentally lower-input, organic cultivation rather than for maximum yield under heavy
fertilisation.

That organic suitability comes with at least one specific disease caveat: net blister blight (もち病)
was significant enough in organically grown Asanoka that researchers developed and published a
pruning-based technique in 2023 specifically to reduce its incidence, since organic management rules
out the fungicide route available to conventional growers. No formal resistance rating for this or any
other disease has been found, so the honest reading is that Asanoka is grown organically in Kagoshima
despite, not because of, an easy disease profile. It is sold primarily as sencha and fukamushi
(deep-steamed) sencha, but at least one specialist single-cultivar retailer also processes it as
kamairicha (pan-fired tea) and as black tea, suggesting real versatility in processing — though sencha
remains its documented primary and registered use.
