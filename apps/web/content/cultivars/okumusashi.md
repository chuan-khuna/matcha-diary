# Okumusashi

Okumusashi is a late-budding, cold-hardy Japanese green tea cultivar bred in Saitama Prefecture
from a controlled cross of Sayamamidori and Yamatomidori, registered in 1962 as Cha Norin No. 26
(茶農林26号). It is one of the earliest cultivars in the Japanese tea registry produced by
deliberate crossing rather than selected from a zairai (landrace, seed-grown) stand — the release
paper itself makes a point of this, noting that until then no Japanese green tea cultivar had been
bred by crossing at all, only black tea. It was developed specifically to extend the growing
season in the Sayama district: alongside Yabukita and Sayamamidori, which both pick in early-to-
mid May, Okumusashi buds and picks roughly ten days later, spreading the harvest and reducing
frost risk for growers working the cooler, comparatively northern Kanto climate.

## Lineage

- **Sayamamidori** (さやまみどり) ♀ — the seed parent. An early Saitama cultivar, itself selected
  from zairai seedlings and named around 1953; in the Okumusashi release paper it appears,
  alongside Yabukita, as one of only two named cultivars then planted in any quantity in Saitama.
- **Yamatomidori** (やまとみどり) ♂ — the pollen parent. Selected in Nara, according to the
  Japanese Tea Sommelier's registry list, from Nara zairai seed; in the Okumusashi trial data it is
  the latest-picking and most cold-hardy of the four varieties compared, the trait Okumusashi was
  crossed to inherit. It does not appear to have had a wide cultivation history of its own outside
  its use as a breeding parent and, later, as a genetic-resource line in tea-breeding research (it
  has since turned up in studies of tea polyploidy and self-incompatibility genotypes).

This is a controlled cross, not a landrace selection: the seed and pollen parents were deliberately
combined by hand at a named experiment station, and the resulting seedling population was
individually selected over roughly fifteen years before the line was fixed and registered. No
descendants of Okumusashi are documented in the sources consulted, and it does not appear as a
parent anywhere in the Japanese Tea Sommelier's master cultivar list.

## History

The cross was made in 1947 (Showa 22) at the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry's Kumagaya
Agricultural Improvement Experiment Station, Toyooka trial site, using Sayamamidori as the seed
parent and Yamatomidori as the pollen parent. Crossing and the first round of individual selection
were led by Sanui Hajime, assisted by Ozawa Keiji, Fuchinoue Yasumoto and Tanaka Mankichi. F1 seed
collected the following year was sown in 1948, and from the germinated population the researchers
began tracking, from 1949, individuals with blue-tinted young shoots and strong root and shoot
growth.

A single line was fixed as the first-selection tree in 1950 and moved to the main field in 1951 to
begin a second round of individual selection. That same year the Kumagaya station was abolished,
and the trial — by then a Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry-designated tea-breeding programme —
was transferred to the newly established Saitama Prefectural Tea Research Institute (埼玉県茶業
研究所), which carried the line forward and eventually authored the release paper. Selection
continued there from 1952, and in 1954 the line was confirmed as the second-selection tree, given
the working strain name 22-131, and switched to vegetative (clonal) propagation for formal
line-comparison trials. It was planted out for full comparison in 1956, with yield, quality and
cold resistance as the trial's main measures.

Results were reviewed at the 1959 national tea-trial research liaison conference, where the line
was approved as a registration candidate; the following years were spent bulking up planting
stock. It was registered in 1962 as the green tea cultivar Okumusashi, Cha Norin No. 26, with
Matsumoto Takeo — who had taken over as the project's lead by then — as first author of the
release paper, alongside Fuchinoue, Yonemaru Tadashi and Tanaka, all of whom had stayed with the
line since the Kumagaya days.

The release paper's afterword makes a claim worth taking at face value: that in black tea, most
Japanese cultivars were already produced by crossing, but that until Okumusashi no Japanese green
tea cultivar had been — every green tea cultivar to that point had been a single-plant selection
out of a zairai stand, Yabukita included. Whether or not it was literally the first, the authors
framed Okumusashi as a proof of concept, that crossing could produce a workable green tea cultivar
and not just a zairai selection, and as filling a specific gap in the Kanto growing calendar: with
Yabukita, Sayamamidori and now Okumusashi, growers in Saitama had an early, a medium and a late
cultivar to spread their harvest and labour across.

## Characteristics

Okumusashi is a late-budding cultivar: in the release paper's multi-year trials it picked roughly
ten days after Yabukita and four to five days after Sayamamidori, placing it mid-rank among the
late varieties available at the time. Growth habit is upright and the plant grows large, with
branches somewhat thicker than Yabukita's and shoots that sit slightly higher on the bush. Mature
leaves are a dark, glossy green — noticeably deeper in colour than Yabukita — elliptical rather
than long-oval, and slightly smaller in area. Vigour is strong and the paper records no notable
pest or disease damage through the trial period, though fruit-set (結実性) is low. Yield ran
somewhat behind Yabukita in the 1962 comparison: 82.8 kg/10a of leaf across first and second flush
(one-bud-three-leaf picking) against Yabukita's 89.2 kg/10a.

Cold hardiness is the cultivar's defining trait and the reason it was bred at all. Osmotic
penetration-value testing (浸透価, measured in atmospheres) showed only a modest edge over
Yabukita — 25.8 to 28.7 against Yabukita's 23.8 to 28.3, across three winters from 1957 to 1959 —
but field observation told a sharper story: Okumusashi showed almost no leaf drop or cold injury
where Yabukita did, and, the difference the paper leans on hardest, it came through abnormal
winter dry spells with barely any desiccation damage while Yabukita suffered considerably. On the
strength of that, the authors recommended it for Saitama's Sayama tea district and the wider Kanto
region generally, rather than for Shizuoka or districts further south or west.

On the cup, the trial's tasting panel scored Okumusashi's leaf shape as slightly inferior to
Yabukita's — the thicker leaf and coarser internodes mark it down on appearance — but rated its
aroma and taste a little higher in both flushes (94.2 against 92.9 points for first flush, 86.0
against 83.5 for second, on the paper's own 100-point scorecard). Aroma was singled out as a
particular strength, described as highly fragrant. All of this was recorded for sencha; the paper
does not address tencha, gyokuro or other processing styles, and none of the specialist cultivar
guides or vendor references consulted for this record mention Okumusashi being grown for anything
else, or, for that matter, mention it at all. Outside its own registration paper and the master
registry list, it appears to be a largely forgotten cultivar today.
