# Sato-wase

Sato-wase is an early-budding sencha cultivar selected by an independent grower, Satō Mitsuteru,
from a seedling of a strain called Abe No. 1, and registered in 1986 under Japan's Seed and
Seedling Law. It never went through the national or prefectural breeding-station pipeline that
produced most of the cultivars in this collection: it carries no MAFF Cha Norin number, and its
own parent, Abe No. 1, is not documented in any source found. What can be said with more
confidence, from a single agronomic survey, is that it buds early and grows with an unusually
upright ("tachiba") leaf habit — the trait that survey found correlates with higher yield through
better light interception. Outside that survey it is almost invisible in the literature: absent
from every major cultivar reference site and retailer checked, and known today mainly through a
handful of specialty-tea blog posts describing small-batch, single-cultivar teas.

## Lineage

- **Abe No. 1 (阿部1号)** — an unregistered strain. It carries no MAFF Cha Norin number and no
  Plant Variety Protection Act registration, and nothing beyond the name survives in the sources
  consulted for this record: not who selected it, where, or from what stock.

Sato-wase is therefore a single-plant selection, not a controlled cross. The naming pattern — a
name plus a serial number, standing for an informally distinguished individual tree or line that
was never itself registered — matches other unregistered "feeder" strains used as seed sources
for later-registered cultivars, such as the "Sugiyama-Yaeho" seed that gave rise to Marishi. No
descendants of Sato-wase are documented in any source found.

## History

Sato-wase was registered in 1986 under the Seed and Seedling Law (品種登録), credited to the
grower Satō Mitsuteru. This is confirmed independently by three sources: My Japanese Green Tea's
cultivar list places it in its "Seed and Seedling Law Registered Cultivars" table; the Ichikawaen
Tea Museum's own registry page lists さとう早生 under 種苗法登録品種 rather than under 農林水産省
命名登録品種 (the MAFF-named category that carries a Cha Norin number); and Minorien's compiled
list of all Cha Norin registrations — a continuous run from 茶農林1号 (Beni-homare, 1953) to
茶農林52号 (Miyama-kaori, 2003) — has no entry for it at all. Unlike a station-bred cultivar such
as Meiryoku, which was registered in two stages (a 1986 MAFF name registration followed by a 1987
Cha Norin number), Sato-wase's only public registration is the 1986 Seed and Seedling Law entry.
This places it alongside contemporaries such as Takane-wase and Inaguchi — individually named
selections registered by private growers in the 1980s, rather than cultivars developed through
the national or prefectural designated-trial programme.

The name follows a pattern common to that group: 早生 (wase) is the plain word for an
early-budding cultivar, and "Sato" most likely honours the breeder's own surname rather than a
place. A 2014 tea-shop blog post about a pouchong-style tea made from Sato-wase, sold under the
name 翡翠 (Hisui, "jade") for the jade-like colour of its new leaf, credits the same producer with
黄金みどり (Kogane-midori, "Golden Green"), another small-production cultivar-based tea — a
pairing that reads as one grower's own introductions being sold under their own names, in keeping
with the cultivar's likely origin as a private selection. That post associates the tea with
諸子沢, a place name in Shizuoka, which is the best geographic anchor found for this record;
Sato-wase's prefecture of origin is not stated outright in any source consulted, and is inferred
from this association rather than from a registration record. A separate blog, from a tea shop in
Tanba, mentions tasting a Sato-wase processed as an oolong and rates it warmly, describing it as
a non-commercial (非売品) tea — evidence that it survives today mainly as raw material for small,
experimental single-cultivar teas rather than as a commodity sencha.

No release paper, characteristics bulletin, or prefectural recommended-cultivar listing for
Sato-wase was found in the sources searched, including J-STAGE's 茶業研究報告 and the MAFF variety
registration database (whose search interface could not be queried directly). The only
quantitative data located comes from a single agronomic survey run for an unrelated purpose (see
Characteristics, below).

## Characteristics

Sato-wase's own name states its most reliable characteristic: 早生 (wase) means early-budding.
The only measured confirmation of this comes from a 2011 paper by Tanaka Junichi, comparing leaf
morphology and yield across roughly 70 Japanese tea cultivars and breeding lines held at NARO's
Makurazaki tea research station in Kagoshima. In a 2009 sub-trial of eight cultivars selected for
a yield comparison, Sato-wase was picked on 11 April — earlier than all seven other cultivars in
that trial, including Izumi (23 April) and Sayamakaori (21 April). No source gives an exact figure
for how many days ahead of Yabukita it buds.

The same paper measured Sato-wase's leaf blade inclination angle at 48.2° (n = 74 leaves), well
above the survey-wide average of 39.1° across all ~70 cultivars and strains, and the seventh
largest angle recorded (sixth among green-tea entries, since the one ahead of it, Beni-homare, is
a black-tea cultivar). A large leaf angle means an upright, "tachiba" growth habit rather than a
sprawling one, and the paper's central finding was that this trait correlates with greater visible
leaf area and, in turn, higher yield — a relationship it also held for Sato-wase specifically: in
the same eight-cultivar yield trial, its spring-flush yield of 989 kg/10a was third-highest,
behind only Izumi (1,353 kg/10a) and Sayamakaori (1,067 kg/10a), and comfortably ahead of every
cultivar in the trial's "small leaf-angle" group. That figure comes from one field in one season
at a research station outside Sato-wase's presumed home region, so it should be read as an
indication of agronomic potential rather than a settled yield figure.

Its registered use is sencha (緑茶・煎茶); no source gives disease resistance, cold hardiness, or
standard tasting notes for it in that role. The only flavour information found concerns
processing choices outside the mainstream: a Shizuoka tea shop makes a lightly oxidised,
pouchong-style tea from it, sold as 翡翠 for the jade colour of its new growth, and a separate
retailer has tasted a Sato-wase oolong and described it approvingly. Whether it performs
comparably well processed as ordinary sencha is not documented anywhere found for this record.
