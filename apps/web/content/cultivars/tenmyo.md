# Tenmyo

Tenmyo is a mid-season Japanese tea cultivar bred at the Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research
Institute, selected in 1977 from open-pollinated seedlings of Samidori and registered in
2006. Unlike most of Kyoto's showcase tencha cultivars, which are grown and hand-picked in
small, intensive plots, Tenmyo was bred explicitly for mechanical harvesting: the institute's
stated aim was a cultivar that could lift the quality of machine-picked tencha, a role
Yabukita had mostly been left to fill by default. It buds within a day or two of Yabukita,
takes shading colour and aroma well, and is processed into tencha, matcha and, to a lesser
extent, gyokuro.

## Lineage

- **Samidori** (さみどり) — the seed parent. Samidori is itself an unregistered but
  foundational Uji cultivar, selected from landrace tea bushes by Koyama Masajiro and used at
  the Kyoto institute as a base population for further selection. Tenmyo is one of the natural
  hybrid seedlings grown from its open-pollinated seed.
- **Pollen parent** — unrecorded. Because the seedling arose from open pollination in a mixed
  planting rather than a controlled cross, the male parent was never identified and cannot be
  recovered now.

This is a single-plant selection from an open-pollinated seedling population, not a
controlled cross: Tenmyo's breeders chose the best individual out of many Samidori-seed
seedlings rather than deliberately combining two named parents. Its true sibling is
**Houshun** — strain "53-7" against Tenmyo's "53-38" — selected from the same 1977 batch of
Samidori seedlings at the same institute and registered in the same year. The two diverged
sharply from there: Houshun was carried forward as an ultra-early gyokuro cultivar, while
Tenmyo was developed as a medium-season, machine-harvestable tencha cultivar. No further
descendants of Tenmyo are documented in the sources consulted.

## History

The Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research Institute breeds original Uji cultivars by two routes:
selecting single plants out of Uji zairai (landrace) stands collected from around the
prefecture, and crossbreeding using existing Uji cultivars as seed parents. Tenmyo and
Houshun both came from a third, intermediate route — open-pollinated seedlings grown from
Samidori seed, from which the institute began selecting promising individuals in 1977 (Showa
52). Tenmyo carried the working strain name "53-38" through the trial period.

The two cultivars were registered together in 2006 (Heisei 18) and announced jointly by the
institute under the title "About the new cultivars Houshun and Tenmyo". The institute framed
Houshun as an ultra-early gyokuro cultivar and Tenmyo as a "high-quality, high-yield tencha
cultivar" (良質・多収てん茶用品種), noting three points in Tenmyo's favour: strong vigour and
good stock spread; upright new shoots that suit mechanical picking; and bright-green leaf
that readily takes on a good shading aroma. The institute's own framing was pointed about the
purpose: for machine-harvested tencha specifically, Tenmyo was expected to bring a
substantial quality improvement over Yabukita, which growers had been using for that role
largely for lack of a better-suited alternative. Kyoto's Uji Tea Trade Association's
subsequent cultivar guide still describes Asahi, Samidori and Ujihikari as the prefecture's
primary hand-picked tencha cultivars, with Tenmyo positioned separately as the institute's
answer to the growing share of tencha picked by machine.

The Uji Tea Trade Association's English-language cultivar page glosses Tenmyo's name simply
as "Exhibition" — evidently a literal rendering of 展 ("to display") and 茗 ("tea") rather
than a romanisation — but no source found explains why the characters were chosen.

## Characteristics

Tenmyo is a medium-season cultivar: its budding and picking period runs, like Samidori's, one
to two days later than Yabukita. That places it close to the middle of the calendar this
collection uses, and a long way from Houshun's ultra-early budding despite the two having been
selected from the same seedling population in the same year — a reminder that budding time is
not conserved even between siblings drawn from one open-pollinated batch.

The bush carries strong vigour and a good, broad stock spread, similar to or exceeding
Samidori. Its new shoots grow upright, the trait the institute bred it for, since an upright
flush is what allows a mechanical harvester to take a clean, even pick. New and mature leaves
are oval, and both larger and thinner than Samidori's. First-flush yield is high and on a par
with Samidori, but the yield is carried differently: Tenmyo is a bud-weight type (heavier
individual buds), where its sibling Houshun is a bud-number type (more buds per unit area).

Processing quality is reported as roughly equal to Samidori and Asahi, the two elite hand-
picked Uji tencha cultivars, which is a high bar. Tenmyo's particular strength is external
colour — it dyes well and evenly on rolling — and it readily develops the "covering aroma"
(覆い香) that shaded tencha is judged on, without requiring the more labour-intensive hand-
picking regime those other cultivars are usually given. Vendor descriptions of the finished
matcha are consistent with this: strong umami, mild bitterness and astringency, a bright
green liquor, and a smooth, faintly marine or "seaweed" note characteristic of well-shaded
tea. Several growers also report it making a good gyokuro, though tencha and matcha remain
its primary use.

Disease resistance data is thinner than for the agronomic figures above: one specialist
reference reports Tenmyo as weak against anthracnose and only moderately resistant to gray
blight (ring spot disease), but this was not corroborated in the institute's own material,
which does not address disease resistance for either Tenmyo or Houshun.

Two decades after registration, Tenmyo remains a minor cultivar in commercial terms. It solved
a specific problem — quality tencha off a mechanical harvester — rather than aiming to
displace the hand-picked cultivars at the top of the Uji tencha hierarchy, and single-cultivar
Tenmyo matcha is still marketed by only a handful of specialist farms, mostly around Wazuka
and Ujitawara.
