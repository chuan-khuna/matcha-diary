# Houshun

Almost every cultivar bred in the last fifty years exists to push a picking season later — the
おく- prefixed ones say so in their names. Houshun goes the other way. It is 極早生, extremely
early: budding and plucking run seven days ahead of Samidori and ten days ahead of Yabukita, which
makes it the earliest thing in the Uji districts and a way of opening the shaded-tea season rather
than stretching its tail.

## Lineage

- **Samidori** ♀ (さみどり) — the recorded seed parent, by open pollination. No pollen parent was
  ever identified, and in an open-pollinated seed lot none could be.

The Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research Institute began growing out natural-cross seedlings from
Samidori bushes in 1977 and selected two lines from the programme: strain 53-7, which became
Houshun, and strain 53-38, which became Tenmyo. Both were registered in February 2006, more than
fifty years after Samidori's own promotion. They share a mother and possibly a father — nobody
knows — so they are half-siblings at minimum.

That makes Houshun a third-generation Uji landrace selection: Uji zairai to Samidori to Houshun,
with the pollen side unrecorded at both steps.

## History

Samidori was designated a Kyoto recommended cultivar in 1954. Twenty-three years later the
Research Institute started collecting its seed, and twenty-nine years after that the two
selections from that seed were registered. It is a long programme by any standard, and a slow one
by the standards of the national stations, whose crosses of the same era took twenty years rather
than thirty.

The pairing of the two releases is deliberate and complementary. Houshun is the extremely early
gyokuro cultivar; Tenmyo is a mid-season cultivar bred for machine-harvested tencha. One prefecture,
one mother plant, two ends of the problem.

## Characteristics

The season is the cultivar. Ten days ahead of Yabukita and a week ahead of its own mother is a
large gap, and it is what a Uji grower is buying.

Yield is equal to Samidori's and high, with a structure the Institute describes as 芽数型 — the
crop comes from many buds rather than heavy ones, which suits hand-picking and the fine plucking
that gyokuro takes. The bush is vigorous with a large spread (樹勢が強く株張りが大きい).

In the cup the Institute credits it with strong aroma and a notable freshness — 香気が強く、新鮮み
がある — which is the pairing an early cultivar is usually selected for, since aroma is what the
first shoots of the season have most of.

No disease-resistance data was located for this record, which is the main gap in it. For a
cultivar planted only in one prefecture's shaded gardens that may simply never have been published.
