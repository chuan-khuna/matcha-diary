# Yamanoibuki

Yamanoibuki (山の息吹, "breath of the mountain") is an early-budding sencha cultivar selected
from open-pollinated Yabukita seed at the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station and
registered in 1997. It exists to answer a specific competitive problem: Kagoshima's early-budding
Yutakamidori reaches the shincha (new-season tea) market before Shizuoka's mid-season Yabukita
crop is ready, and Shizuoka's own tea industry council promoted Yamanoibuki as one of a handful of
"strategic cultivars" meant to close that gap without sacrificing cup quality — an early cultivar
whose water colour, aroma and taste hold up rather than the thinner, greener liquor early
cultivars often give.

## Lineage

- **Yabukita seed** — an individual selection grown from Yabukita's own open-pollinated seed at
  the Shizuoka station, not a cutting of Yabukita itself and not the product of a controlled
  cross.

Because Yamanoibuki was raised from seed rather than crossed deliberately, its pollen parent was
never controlled and is not recorded. What is documented is the seed parent: an open-pollinated
Yabukita mother plant at the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station, its flowers fertilised
by whatever pollen reached them from a neighbouring bush. This is the same structural gap that
applies to Yabukita's own origin from a Zairai stand, one generation further down the tree — and
it puts Yamanoibuki in the same category as Sayamakaori, another Yabukita open-pollinated seedling
selected at a different prefectural station four decades earlier. No cultivar bred from Yamanoibuki
itself, and no notable siblings from the same seed lot, were found in the sources consulted.

## History

Yamanoibuki was selected at the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station (静岡県茶業試験場) —
the institution, since reorganised into the Tea Research Center of the Shizuoka Prefectural
Research Institute of Agriculture and Forestry, that also bred Okuhikari and Meiryoku among others.
It was named and registered in 1997 under Japan's Plant Variety Protection Act (種苗法) rather than
as a numbered Cha Norin (茶農林) cultivar: a full table of the 52 Cha Norin registrations issued
between 1953 and 2003 — a range that spans 1997 on both sides — contains no entry for Yamanoibuki,
while both the Japanese Tea Sommelier's master cultivar registry and My Japanese Green Tea's
cultivar list separately confirm a 1997 Seed and Seedling Law registration. No exact registration
number was found in any source consulted, and no selection year distinct from the 1997 registration
date is sourced either, so both are left unstated here rather than guessed. This registration path
mirrors Okuhikari's own a decade earlier, and Shizuoka Prefecture's broader habit of registering
its home-bred cultivars directly rather than through the nationally coordinated Cha Norin trial
series that produced releases like Meiryoku at the separate, national station in Kanaya.

Shizuoka's own tea industry council later listed Yamanoibuki, alongside Okuhikari, as one of a
small number of cultivars it was actively promoting to growers in its cultivar guidebook, under
the heading "tea cultivars to promote from here on" (今後推進すべき茶品種). The stated goal, in the
council's own wording, was a stable supply of high-quality early-season tea able to outdo the
quality of Kagoshima's Yutakamidori in the new-tea market — positioning Yamanoibuki specifically as
an early-harvest-region answer to Kyushu's head start each spring. A companion piece from the same
institute, reprinted in the trade journal *Cha* (published by the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Industry
Council) and signed 中村順行, sets out the cultivar's agronomic and quality characteristics in
detail — the source behind most of the specifics below.

Since registration, Yamanoibuki has continued to appear as one of a fixed panel of Shizuoka's
officially recommended cultivars (静岡県茶奨励品種) in agronomic research from the same institute's
successor: a 2015 study of first-flush shoot growth and hardening, and a 2022 study of intensive
shading for white-leaf tea (白葉茶) production, both discussed under Characteristics below.

## Characteristics

Yamanoibuki buds four to five days earlier than Yabukita, placing it among the early cultivars.
The tree form is slightly upright with extremely strong vigour; mature leaves are a slightly
short-tipped ellipse, green in colour. Bud uniformity at picking time is good, though hundred-bud
weight runs slightly light. A 2015 comparative trial against Yabukita, Tsuyuhikari, Okuhikari and
Koushun found its new-shoot unfolding speed roughly level with Yabukita's — despite budding
earlier, it does not race through leaf expansion — and its shoots hardened relatively quickly at
the optimal four-to-five-leaf picking stage, behind only Okuhikari among the cultivars compared.
Yield is reported as comparable to Yabukita's. Cold hardiness against red-scorch frost injury is
strong, and anthracnose resistance is somewhat better than Yabukita's, but resistance to stem/root
rot (立枯れ症) and to the white peach scale insect (クワシロカイガラムシ) is no better than
Yabukita's own middling-to-weak level. Being an early cultivar, it still needs full frost
protection and is best suited to warm districts with low frost risk; its vigorous growth and
comparatively strong anthracnose resistance are reported to give it a wider adaptable range than
the older Shizuoka early cultivar Oiwase (おおいわせ), within those warm-district limits. Vigorous,
upright growth also means young plantings need careful training to build stool width rather than
height.

In the cup, the breeding station's own account is candid that this is unusual for an early
cultivar: water colour, aroma and taste are rated excellent rather than merely acceptable. The
aroma is light and fresh, suggestive of new greenery, with substantial umami and comparatively
little astringency — consistent with a chemical profile high in umami-associated amino acids and
theanine and low in astringency-associated catechins. Processing notes from the same source
recommend roughly 30 seconds of steaming for the better shape and appearance, but roughly 45
seconds for the better aroma, colour and taste — a genuine trade-off between form and flavour
rather than a single optimal setting. Commercially it is worked mainly as ordinary and deep-steamed
(fukamushi) sencha and as kamairicha (pan-fired tea), and one specialist single-cultivar retailer
also sells it as black tea; tasting notes from that retailer describe a rounded, smooth mouthfeel
with a subtle, refined aroma and a clean finish, rating it low in astringency and moderate in both
umami and body.

A 2022 trial of intensive (100%) shading for white-leaf tea (白葉茶) production, run across twelve
of Shizuoka's recommended cultivars, found Yamanoibuki's leaf colour bleached out particularly
strongly under shade — its SPAD value fell below Yabukita's, among the small group of cultivars
(with Saemidori, Meiryoku, Fushun and Benifuuki) that whitened the most — and its crude-tea
(aracha) quality in sensory evaluation surpassed Yabukita's, on a par with Saemidori and Koushun.
The same study did not rank it among the higher-yielding cultivars under that specific shading
regime, and its authors recommended it, alongside Saemidori and Yabukita, for shaded production
prioritising quality and leaf colour over yield rather than for a heavy-shade programme aimed at
volume. This points at a real, if experimental and not yet commercially established, potential for
Yamanoibuki beyond the unshaded sencha it is normally grown for.
