# Narino

Narino is a matcha and tencha cultivar belonging to a single garden. It was mass-selected, not
bred by cross, out of the seed-grown (zairai) tea trees of Horii Shichimeien's Okunoyama garden
in Uji, and registered under Japan's Plant Variety Protection Act in 2002. Unlike almost every
other cultivar in this collection, it has no prefectural or national breeding programme behind
it — it does not appear on Kyoto Prefecture's table of recommended Uji cultivars or in NARO's own
handbook of nationally released tea cultivars — and, as far as we have been able to determine,
it is grown nowhere but Okunoyama and sold by no one but Horii Shichimeien. Its defining trait
is compositional: a theanine content Horii states runs close to double that of conventional
matcha cultivars.

## Lineage

- **Uji zairai seedling, Okunoyama garden (奥ノ山茶園)** — a mass selection from a seed-grown,
  non-clonal population, not a controlled cross. No pollen parent is recorded or recoverable.

This is the same kind of lineage as Yabukita's — an individual pulled out of a genetically mixed
zairai stand — but conducted almost a century later, on the private stock of a single Uji garden
rather than a farmer's cleared field. Its closest relative is **Okunoyama** (奥の山, written with
the hiragana の for the cultivar, distinct from 奥ノ山 with katakana ノ for the garden both were
selected from): a sibling selection made from the same trees in the same 1980–2000 screening,
kept for its dark leaf colour rather than its amino-acid profile. Narino and Okunoyama are
routinely sold and discussed as a pair, but they are two separately registered cultivars with two
different grounds for registration.

## History

Horii Shichimeien, founded in 1879, has stewarded the Okunoyama garden since acquiring it in the
mid-Meiji period. The garden is seed-grown zairai — every tree genetically its own individual —
and Horii's own materials describe it as continuing from the Muromachi period, the last surviving
example of the "Uji Shichimeien," the seven historic tea gardens of Uji. That antiquity is not
only house lore: a 2019 soil-science paper (Inoue et al., *Japanese Journal of Soil Science and
Plant Nutrition*) applied AMS radiocarbon dating to the soil beneath Okunoyama's traditional
straw-and-reed shading beds and put the establishment of that shading system in the first half of
the 15th century — about 150 years earlier than documentary sources had suggested. The dating
covers the shading system itself, not the planting date of any individual tea tree, so it
corroborates the garden's age without directly dating Narino's source trees.

Starting in 1980, the fifth-generation head, Horii Nobuo, spent twenty years working through the
garden's zairai trees to find individuals worth carrying forward. We could not independently
verify a candidate-narrowing count or a single selection year for that process beyond Horii's own
description of it as a twenty-year screening; what is documented is the outcome — two named
selections, Narino and Okunoyama — and the basis on which Narino was registered: component
analysis of its leaf showed theanine at close to double the level of conventional cultivars, and
Horii states that measurement was what carried the registration. Narino was registered in 2002
under the Plant Variety Protection Act (種苗法), a route distinct from both the MAFF Cha Norin
national trial system (Narino is absent from NARO's own cultivar handbook of nationally released
selections) and a prefectural recommended-cultivar listing (it is likewise absent from Kyoto
Prefecture's official table of Uji 茶奨励品種). It is registered and marketed as a proprietary,
single-garden cultivar. A more selectively picked and processed grade is sold alongside the
standard line as Premium Narino (プレミアム成里乃).

## Characteristics

Budding time is recorded as medium (中生) by the Japanese cultivar reference 日本茶備忘録; no
figure in days relative to Yabukita, no yield data and no disease-resistance ratings appear to
have been published for Narino, which is unsurprising for a cultivar with no institutional trial
record behind it.

Narino is grown and processed exclusively as tencha for matcha. At Okunoyama it is picked as the
first flush, by hand, after 40 days under tana (trellis) shading with kanreisha netting — longer
than the 20–30 days typical of shaded sencha, though within the range used for tencha and
gyokuro — before stone-grinding.

The trait the cultivar is registered for is compositional rather than morphological: Horii
Shichimeien states Narino's theanine — the amino acid behind matcha's umami — runs close to
double the level of conventional cultivars, while the tea shows comparatively little of the
bitterness and astringency that level would normally bring with it. In the cup that shows up as
pronounced sweetness and a heavy, rounded umami; Horii's own marketing describes it as drinkable
on its own, without the wagashi usually paired with a strong matcha. That is the producer's
characterisation rather than an independently published sensory analysis, and should be read
accordingly — but it is consistent with the theanine figure being the one component of Narino's
profile that was documented well enough to register the cultivar in the first place.
