# Minamisayaka

Minamisayaka is a Miyazaki sencha cultivar bred from a 1970 cross and registered in 1991. It sits
a little outside the normal Yabukita-descended family tree that dominates this collection: through
its seed-parent line it carries a measured share of Assam (Camellia sinensis var. assamica) and
Caucasus-origin tea material, inherited from a breeding programme's use of exotic germplasm two
breeding generations before Minamisayaka's own 1970 cross. That ancestry shows up directly in the
cup — tasters and the cultivar's own release material describe a milky, floral aroma unlike almost
anything else in the Yabukita lineage — and it is a large part of why growers reach for Minamisayaka
specifically for pan-roasted (kamairicha) and black tea rather than treating it as a general-purpose
sencha cultivar.

## Lineage

- **Miya A-6 (宮A6)** ♀ — bred by Miyazaki Prefecture; itself a cross of **Takachiho** (たかちほ,
  a selection from Miyazaki Zairai seedlings) and **Miya F1-9-4-48** (宮F1-9-4-48), a Miyazaki
  breeding-line F1 hybrid.
- **Chamoto F1 NN27 (茶本F1NN27)** ♂ — bred at the National Tea Research Station (国立茶業試験場)
  from **Yabukita** and **Shizuoka Zairai 16** (静在16), a Shizuoka landrace seedling. The same
  clonal line was later used, the other way round, as the seed parent of Sakimidori.

The unusual part of the pedigree sits one generation further back, on Miya A-6's paternal side.
Miya F1-9-4-48 was itself bred from **MAi23** (an Assam-variety selection) as seed parent and
**MC3** (a Caucasus-origin selection) as pollen parent — both documented by name in NARO's own
pedigree chart. That makes Minamisayaka's great-grandparents, on its mother's father's side, an
Assam tea plant and a Caucasus tea plant respectively: unusual company for a cultivar that is
otherwise raised and sold as an ordinary Japanese sencha. No source consulted explains why a
Caucasus-origin parent specifically was used in this cross, and the author of the most detailed
English-language account of it says plainly that they could not find out either. The Assam
connection is less mysterious in context: Japan ran several mid-twentieth-century breeding
programmes that imported Assam and other foreign material for black tea development, and the same
NARO chart shows an unrelated Assam-descended parent behind Benifuuki.

Minamisayaka is a controlled cross, not a landrace selection, and every plant of it is a clone of
the individual selected from that 1970 cross. Miya A-6 was also used, independently, to breed the
Unkai (雲海) cultivar from the same pair of grandparents (Takachiho × Miya F1-9-4-48) — a different
seedling was selected in that case, so Unkai and Minamisayaka's female parent are full siblings
rather than the same plant. No descendants of Minamisayaka itself are recorded in the sources
consulted.

## History

Miya A-6 and Chamoto F1 NN27 were crossed in 1970. The resulting seedlings were raised and
evaluated at a Miyazaki prefectural tea research station for roughly two decades — sources give
the institution's English name inconsistently ("Miyazaki Prefectural Agricultural Experiment
Station" and "Miyazaki Agricultural Research Institute" both appear), and none gives its precise
contemporary Japanese name.

The best individual from that trial was named and registered in 1991 under MAFF's national
tea-cultivar naming system, becoming, by the account of myjapanesegreentea.com, "tea cultivar
number 42" — Cha Norin No. 42. NARO's own handbook separately records a later, different kind of
registration: on 14 March 1994, Minamisayaka was registered under the Plant Variety Registration
Act (種苗法) as variety No. 3932, a scheme that assigns sequential numbers across all registered
crops rather than tea specifically (compare Benifuuki's unrelated No. 4591 in the same table). The
two registrations are easy to conflate, and are recorded separately in the frontmatter of this
record; see the `conflicts` note there.

NARO's handbook gives the naming rationale directly: bred in Miyazaki, suited to warm regions, and
carrying a refreshing aroma, it was named Minamisayaka — written 南爽, "south" and "clear/fresh" —
though the registered name itself is kana only.

Minamisayaka has stayed a minor cultivar outside Miyazaki. It is one of the roughly eighty
cultivars Shinkoju, a Japanese specialist single-cultivar tea shop, stocks and sells separately by
processing style; one of their own lots is grown not in Miyazaki at all but in Sayama, Saitama,
which the shop calls out explicitly as a point of interest — a Miyazaki-bred cultivar producing
tea more than a thousand kilometres from where it was raised.

## Characteristics

Budding is medium to slightly late: NARO's handbook puts first picking one to three days after
Yabukita, and 日本茶備忘録 classes it as 中生 (mid-season) and specifically suited to low-pesticide
and organic cultivation. Growth habit is a strongly upright (直立型) form, with vigour similar to
Yabukita. Leaves are elliptical, slightly larger than Yabukita's and similar in thickness, dark
green with a visible lustre. Cuttings root and establish well.

Yield runs ahead of Yabukita at both flushes — 10 to 20% more at first flush (ichibancha) and 30
to 40% more at second flush (nibancha) — though no absolute kg/10a figure was found in the sources
consulted. It carries strong resistance to anthracnose, ring spot disease and white peach scale,
needing no routine control for any of the three. Set against that, it is susceptible to red blight
and to mochi disease (blister blight), both of which need active management in areas where they
occur, and growers have reported cases of aokugare (青枯れ, a cold-related dieback) in cold
districts, so the cultivar's "can be grown nationwide" billing comes with a caveat for the coldest
sites. Processing notes from NARO flag that its buds are soft relative to their apparent maturity —
rough-rolling (粗揉) with too little leaf in the drum can cause the leaf to pool at the bottom of
the machine — that lighter steaming brings out its distinctive aroma more than deep steaming does,
and that it is a "bud-weight" type that punishes late picking.

The cup is where the unusual ancestry shows. NARO's own characterisation is a floral aroma
"reminiscent of milk tea" (ミルクティーを想起させる) over a clean, light taste. Shinkoju's tasting
notes track the same theme across processing styles: its standard sencha is described as having
"the multilayered aroma and flavour of a Southeast Asian tea"; its fukamushi sencha as unusually
"juicy" for a green tea; its kamairicha (pan-roasted) as having "a transparent floral aroma and
balanced flavour"; and its black tea as "flowery and clear, with a sweet, coconut-milk-like
aftertaste." That aroma profile is also, by most accounts, a point of genuine disagreement rather
than universal praise — myjapanesegreentea.com notes that Minamisayaka's flavour is different
enough from Yabukita that Japanese tea professionals' opinions of it are divided. It is not,
on this evidence, a cultivar aiming to be a better all-purpose sencha; it is a cultivar built
around one distinctive, polarising aroma, and it is put to its best use where that aroma is the
point — pan-roasted tea and, especially, black tea.
