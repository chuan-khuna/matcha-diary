# Yaeho

Yaeho is an early-budding sencha cultivar registered in 1954 as Cha Norin No. 17, selected from
Shizuoka Zairai stock by Sugiyama Hikosaburo — the same grower who, decades earlier and a few
kilometres away, picked out the seedling that became Yabukita. It never took anything like
Yabukita's share of the country's tea gardens; today it covers a vanishingly small fraction of
Japan's plantings and is rarely sold as a single-cultivar tea. What keeps it in the record is its
lineage: it is the documented seed parent of Ooiwase, one of the few early cultivars bred
specifically to work a staggered harvest alongside Yabukita, and — by a connection this record
traces out below, with the caveats it deserves — an ancestor of Marishi, a cultivar now prized for
a strikingly clear green liquor.

## Lineage

- **Shizuoka Zairai individual** — selected by Sugiyama Hikosaburo from his own tea garden at a
  locality called Dampira, in what was then Udo village, Abe County, and is now part of Shizuoka
  City; not a controlled cross.

Because it is a landrace selection rather than a cross, Yaeho has no recorded pollen parent. It is
the documented seed parent of Ooiwase (Yaeho × Yabukita, Shizuoka Tea Experiment Station, crossed
1960), and, on a line this record examines in detail below, an ancestor of Marishi, registered
1996.

## History

### Selection and registration

Sugiyama Hikosaburo selected the tree that became Yaeho from his own tea garden at Dampira, in
Udo village, Abe County — the same district, on the outskirts of Shizuoka City, where he had
already found Yabukita and Yabuminami. It was tested from 1935 to 1946 (Showa 10–21) at the
Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station's MAFF-designated foundation-stock garden and adopted
as a Shizuoka Prefecture recommended cultivar. In October 1954, alongside five other cultivars —
Natsumidori, Asagiri, Kiyomidori, Hatsumidori and the black-tea cultivar Benikaori — it was
registered nationally as one of that year's new Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry cultivars,
Cha Norin No. 17, its former strain name given as 八重穂 (Yaeho, in kanji). The registration
bulletin states the naming plainly: it was called Yae-ho, "many-layered ear," because it puts out
unusually many axillary buds.

### Sugiyama Hikosaburo and the "Sugiyama-Yaeho" question

The connection to Sugiyama Hikosaburo is not in doubt — the primary registration record names him
directly as the selector, just as it does for Yabukita. What is genuinely unresolved is a second,
related claim made by present-day Shizuoka tea merchants. A planting said to descend from
Sugiyama's own selection still stands at Maruko, in Shizuoka City's Suruga ward, and local shops
sell it under the name "Sugiyama-Yaeho" (杉山八重穂) — explicitly distinguishing it from the
nationally registered Yaeho. One shop's account, given across two dated posts by its owner in
2005, explains the distinction in detail: Sugiyama-Yaeho reliably throws multiple new shoots from
a single leaf axil — the trait the cultivar was named for — while the registered Yaeho, described
there as a natural seedling selected from Sugiyama-Yaeho, does not reliably show it. A specialist
retailer selling certified-organic Yaeho independently makes the same distinction, stating that
"Sugiyama-Yaeho" is a different cultivar from the registered Yaeho.

This does not square cleanly with the 1954 bulletin, which describes Sugiyama Hikosaburo as having
selected the registered cultivar directly, with no mention of an intervening natural-seedling
generation. Both cannot be taken as simply correct, and no source available for this record
resolves the discrepancy — it may be a matter of two related garden selections Sugiyama made in
different locations (Udo village, which was carried to national registration, and Maruko, which
was not), remembered and marketed differently seventy years later. What both accounts agree on is
that Sugiyama Hikosaburo is the origin point of both plants, and that "Yaeho" in something close to
its present form was current in Shizuoka tea gardens by the mid-1930s.

### Ooiwase and Marishi

Ooiwase was bred at the Shizuoka Tea Experiment Station, using Yaeho as the seed parent and
Yabukita as the pollen parent. The cross was made in October 1960; seed was sown by the end of
1961 and seedlings raised under glass until 1963, when they were planted out. A first selection
was fixed in 1967, propagated by cuttings from 1970, and carried through strain-comparison trials
under the designation 60-2 from 1971 to 1976. The results were strong enough that the Shizuoka
Prefecture Tea Recommended Cultivar Council approved it in January 1977, naming it Ooiwase and
backdating its adoption to the 1976 season. It was never submitted for national Cha Norin
registration — like Yaeho's own path through prefectural recommendation before national listing,
Ooiwase stopped at the prefectural stage.

Marishi has a smaller, more anecdotal history. In the tea garden of the Yamamori family at
Mizumishiro, in the hills above Shizuoka City, an unusually handsome tea bush was found growing
between the rows of a Yaeho planting — spotted, by one retailer's account, by the mother of
Yamamori Miyoshi. Miyoshi and Yamamori Risao propagated and trialled it, and it was registered
under the Plant Variety Protection Act on 18 March 1996 as Registration No. 4953, named Marishi
after Marici, a Buddhist guardian deity. The official registration record describes it as a
natural cross-pollinated seedling selected from Yaeho, extra-early in both budding and harvest at
its Shizuoka City breeding site, and distinguished from Yaeho by longer, more elliptical new
leaves, a darker green colour, more gloss, and higher total nitrogen and amino acid content. Its
first harvest produced only 1.5 kg of finished tea, and its liquor was reportedly so vividly green
that people who saw it for the first time suspected it had been artificially coloured. A related,
unregistered sibling cultivar from the same garden, Mizumishiro-kaori (Sugiyama-Yaeho ×
Inzatsu 131), buds even earlier than Marishi and, like Marishi, inherits the paired-bud trait —
one more thread connecting both back to the Sugiyama-Yaeho line rather than to the registered
clone. When the Fuji branch of the prefectural tea experiment station closed in 2007, cultivation
of Marishi passed by arrangement to Fuji Akiyama-en, which still grows and sells it as its
signature tea.

## Characteristics

Yaeho is an early cultivar. The 1954 registration trial recorded bud-break on 28 March and a
first-flush harvest on 3 May; growth habit is intermediate, vigour strong with an upright-leaning
tendency, and it sets abundant flower buds though fruit-set is not especially high. No source
found gives an exact day-count for Yaeho's own budding time against Yabukita, though its offspring
Ooiwase — which the 1978 release paper describes as inheriting Yaeho's earliness — buds about ten
days and is harvested about five days ahead of Yabukita in side-by-side trials, which gives a
rough sense of where Yaeho itself sits.

Reported yield from the 1954 trial averaged 149.3 kan per 10a (roughly 560 kg by modern
conversion) across the 1943–45 harvests on 9–11-year-old bushes, combining all three flushes.
Propagation is a genuine weak point: in the Shizuoka Tea Experiment Station's side-by-side cutting
trials for the Ooiwase release paper, Yaeho cuttings rooted reasonably well in the short term
(96.7% at two months, against Yabukita's 93.3%) but fell away badly over the following two years —
85.0% survival and a 42.6% successful-seedling rate, against 98.5%/77.7% for Yabukita and
98.2%/72.4% for Ooiwase itself.

Cold hardiness of mature winter leaves is strong, but Yaeho is markedly susceptible to
bark-splitting injury from autumn frost: a 1979 varietal comparison ranked it among the most
tender cultivars tested, and a young planting followed for the 1978 Ooiwase paper recorded injury
in 84.3% of Yaeho trees, with a 22.5% death rate, against 1.2% injury and no deaths for Yabukita in
the same ground. It is also noted as weak against white-star disease. Set against that, a 1985
Shizuoka survey found it, together with Kanayamidori, the most resistant of the surveyed cultivars
to bacterial shoot blight, a disease to which its own offspring Ooiwase proved comparatively
susceptible.

On the chemistry behind the cup, Yaeho runs consistently lower in total and soluble nitrogen than
Yabukita (5.68% against 6.20% total nitrogen; 1.90% against 2.17% soluble nitrogen, three-year
averages from the Ooiwase trial) and higher in tannin (12.78% against 11.94%) — the likely
explanation for a 1972 warm-region comparison trial that rated its made-tea quality inferior to
Yabukita and to several other early cultivars, attributing the shortfall specifically to lower
nitrogen. That is not the whole story, though: growers who still work with it describe a
distinctive chestnut-like aroma, and an unusual brewing behaviour in which low-temperature, longer
steeping brings out a pronounced bitterness that recedes into sweetness, while a quick, near-boiling
steep suppresses the bitterness and pushes the sweetness forward — the reverse of how most
asamushi Tenryu teas are meant to be brewed. It is also considered well suited to gyokuro. Grown
almost nowhere as a monovarietal today, it survives mainly as a rarity in Shizuoka's Tenryu
district, prized specifically for that aroma, and as a certified-organic single-origin planting as
far afield as Tanegashima, Kagoshima — well outside the "Shizuoka and warmer" range its own
registration bulletin recommended.
