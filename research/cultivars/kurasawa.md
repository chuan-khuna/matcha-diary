# Kurasawa

Kurasawa is a Shizuoka sencha cultivar selected in 1967 from open-pollinated Yabukita
seedlings and never registered. It is one of a cluster of strains — retrospectively grouped as
the "Shizu-7000 series" — that the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station pulled out of a
Yabukita-seedling screening programme in the mid-to-late 1960s, and, like most of that cluster,
it stayed a locally-propagated strain rather than a nationally-promoted cultivar. Its two claims
to notice are unrelated to registration status: a heavy, forest-like astringency that carries
through to a distinctive cocoa note when it is processed as matcha, and its role three years
after its own selection as the seed parent of Koshun, the aroma cultivar that did go on to be
registered in 2000.

## Lineage

- **Yabukita** — the seed-bearing parent. Kurasawa was picked out of a population of seedlings
  grown from Yabukita seed that had been pollinated naturally rather than by a controlled cross,
  so its pollen parent was never recorded and cannot now be recovered.

This is a landrace-style single-plant selection, not a controlled cross, and it sits one step
further from a "clean" pedigree than Yabukita itself: Yabukita was picked from a Zairai
seed-grown stand, but Kurasawa was picked from a Yabukita seed-grown stand, so it is already a
second-generation open-pollinated selection with an unrecorded father on both sides of that
history. It was not an isolated project — the same Shizuoka programme, working from Yabukita
seed in the same few years, also produced Yamakai (trial name Shizu-7166), Suruga-wase
(Shizu-7109, 1962), Fuji-midori (Shizu-7224, 1962) and Shizu-7132 (1967), Kurasawa's own trial
name being Shizu-7111. None of these five were ever submitted for national Cha Norin
registration. Kurasawa's clearest documented descendant is Koshun, a controlled cross of
Kurasawa (seed parent) and Kanayamidori (pollen parent, 茶農林30号) made in 1970 and registered
in 2000 — for which see the separate record of that cultivar. No cultivars bred from Kurasawa
other than Koshun have been located.

## History

Kurasawa was selected in 1967 at the Shizuoka Prefectural Tea Experiment Station, one line pulled
from a population of naturally-pollinated Yabukita seedlings under the trial designation
Shizu-7111. A retrospective slide deck on Koshun by the station's own researchers places this
selection inside a documented wave of Shizuoka cultivar work: a 1965–1974 push, driven by rising
domestic demand and larger tea-processing machinery, aimed explicitly at higher-yielding,
differently-timed, higher-quality cultivars, with Kurasawa named alongside Kanayamidori and
Ooiwase as the releases that period produced.

"Released" is doing some work in that sentence, though, because Kurasawa itself never was, in the
formal sense. Checking Kurasawa's name against MAFF's own 附表１茶農林登録品種一覧 — the complete
table of all 52 nationally registered Cha Norin cultivars, from 茶農林1号 (Beni-homare, 1953)
through 茶農林52号 (Miyamakaori, 2003) — finds no entry for it; the same absence holds for its
Shizu-7000 stablemates Yamakai, Suruga-wase, Fuji-midori and Shizu-7132. A separately compiled
registry (the japaneseteasommelier master list) independently classifies all five as
"non-registered" strains, in a table distinct from the one listing cultivars that received formal
name- and cultivar-registration. That same source explains, for one member of the group
specifically, why it was passed over — Shizu-7132 tended to redden on the summer harvest, gave
too dark a liquor, and was too astringent from lowland gardens, despite a prized cherry-leaf
aroma. No source located gives an equivalent explanation for Kurasawa itself; none of the material
consulted states a reason it was not carried forward to national registration, and this record
does not invent one. What can be said is only the observable pattern: it never entered the
prefectural or national comparison-trial pipeline that produces a Cha Norin number, and by 1970 —
three years after its own selection, while it was presumably still being evaluated as a strain in
its own right — the station was already crossing it with the newly registered Kanayamidori to
produce what would become Koshun, whose own path from cross to registration took thirty years and
is unusually well documented by comparison. Kurasawa's practical legacy runs through that cross
more than through any release of its own.

It has nonetheless persisted in cultivation and in the market. It is currently sold as a
standalone single-cultivar tea by Shinkoju (心向樹), a specialist Saitama retailer devoted
entirely to named-cultivar teas, across five quality grades and four processing styles — ordinary
steamed sencha, deep-steamed (fukamushi) sencha, pan-fired kamairicha, and black tea — and the
same retailer sells young Kurasawa plants, meaning it is still being propagated rather than sold
down from old stock. Separately, at least one producer (Houkouen) markets it as a single-estate
matcha, an application distinct from the leaf teas it is more usually described in, and one
retailer's account of that matcha describes it as the only Kurasawa matcha currently on the
Japanese market — a claim made by a single vendor source and not independently corroborated here.

## Characteristics

Kurasawa buds in a normal, mid-season window — a "中生" cultivar in the Japanese sources, echoed
by an English-language source describing it as "normal budding" — but no source gives a specific
day-count relative to Yabukita, the usual convention in this collection; that figure is generated
by the formal comparison trials that a registered cultivar goes through, and Kurasawa did not go
through them. Multiple independent sources agree that it grows vigorously and yields heavily,
again without a specific kg/10a figure attached. No disease-resistance or cold-hardiness data has
been located for it in any source consulted, which is again consistent with a strain that was
never carried through the national or prefectural trial system that would normally produce that
data.

The cup is where the sourcing is most consistent and most specific. Shinkoju's own tasting copy
describes a natural aroma reminiscent of forest and woodland, paired with an astringency that
builds gradually and runs deep ("じわじわと深みの渋味") rather than hitting sharply up front; an
English-language source independently converges on the same pairing, describing "a distinctly
refreshing aroma and intense astringency, which gives the infusion a distinctive depth." That
astringency is pronounced enough that Shinkoju's own five-point in-house scoring rates several of
its Kurasawa grades at or near the top of its astringency scale while rating umami and body only
middling — a tea built around structure and aroma rather than sweetness. As matcha specifically,
one retailer source is unambiguous about what sets it apart: "a unique flavor profile with
noticeable notes of cocoa — rich, deep, and incredibly aromatic," with a dense, velvety texture
it recommends for a matcha latte rather than a thin usucha. That cocoa character, together with
the underlying astringency, is the throughline connecting every source on this cultivar, and is
the clearest reason it is still sought out on its own rather than folded into a blend, sixty years
after a station researcher pulled one seedling out of a Yabukita trial plot and never bothered to
put it through the registration system.
