# Kuritawase

Kuritawase is an extremely early-budding sencha cultivar selected on Tanegashima, the Kagoshima
island that ships Japan's earliest new tea each spring. It exists because a family who migrated
from Shizuoka in 1909 spent nearly two decades hand-selecting the single best plant out of a
seedling field grown from the native Shizuoka seed they had brought with them — work finished, in
its final step, under the guidance of Sugiyama Hikosaburo, the same grower who, two decades
earlier and on the other side of Japan, had selected Yabukita. Where Yabukita became the
mid-season benchmark the rest of the industry is measured against, Kuritawase went the opposite
direction: it buds and is picked roughly ten to thirteen days before Yabukita, and its entire
commercial purpose is to be first to market.

## Lineage

- **Shizuoka zairai (native) seedling** — an individual selection from a seed-grown stand, not a
  controlled cross; it contributes an unknowable pollen parent, and a loose historical link to the
  same wave of Shizuoka native-seed stock that, selected by other hands, also produced Yabukita,
  Koyanishi, Rokuro and Yaeho on the other side of the country.

Because Kuritawase was selected rather than bred, it has no recorded pollen parent, in the same
way Yabukita does not. What is unusual is how well documented its own onward descendants are for a
cultivar this obscure: it is the parent bush of Shoju, a bud sport found on a Kuritawase plant at
Furuta, Tanegashima, and registered under the Plant Variety Protection Act in 1996; and it is the
pollen parent — not the seed parent — of Shizukaori, a controlled cross made in 1989 at the
Shizuoka Tea Experiment Station using Okuhikari as the seed parent, registered in 2015. That a
Tanegashima cultivar ended up supplying pollen to a Shizuoka breeding programme sixty years after
its own founding seed made the opposite journey is one of the odder loops in this collection's
lineage.

## History

### A family migration and a decade of selection

According to a commemorative stone inscription at Furuta Banyamine in Nishinoomote City,
Tanegashima — erected by the Kurita family in November 1984 to mark 75 years since their move to
the island, and transcribed on the local history site furusato-tanegashima.net — the grower Kurita
Mosaburo ordered Shizuoka zairai seed in Meiji 42 (1909) and sowed it in a seedling tea garden at
Furuta. His son, Kurita Seiichi, selected 60 promising individual stocks from that field in Taisho
10 (1921) and carried out further individual surveys of the best of them in 1926. In Showa 2
(1927), Sugiyama Hikosaburo — the tea-cultivar-improvement pioneer who, in 1908 in Shizuoka, had
already selected the plant that became Yabukita — visited Tanegashima, and under his guidance the
family selected a single outstanding stock from among the survivors. Cuttings from that one plant
were propagated, and several dozen were planted at the Kagoshima Prefectural Tea Experiment
Station in 1931. myjapanesegreentea.com's independent account of the same history broadly agrees,
though it credits the 1927 selection to Mosaburo himself rather than to his son Seiichi — see
`conflicts` for how this record resolves that discrepancy.

### Trials and prefectural recognition

Recommended-cultivar trials at the prefectural experiment station ran for eight years, from Showa
27 to Showa 34 (1952–1959), and a comparative sencha-quality trial pitting Kuritawase against
other early cultivars followed in Showa 40 (1965). On the strength of those results, Kuritawase
was designated Kagoshima Prefecture's recommended cultivar (県奨励品種) in Showa 41 (1966) and
spread through the island's early-harvest districts. By the end of fiscal 1983 the monument
records 75 hectares under cultivation. Separate research-journal trials from the 1970s corroborate
this local picture from the outside: a two-part varietal comparison run at Makurazaki, on the
Kagoshima mainland, in 1972, 1973 and 1975 tested Kuritawase's first-crop quality against Yabukita
and several other early cultivars (Yutakamidori, Surugawase, Makinoharawase, Hatsumidori, Yaeho)
and concluded that Kuritawase was "the most suitable variety from the viewpoints of both better
quality of the made tea and the earliest production among the early varieties in the warm region
of southern Kyushu" — a research-station verdict that lines up with the prefecture's own decision
the following year. Kuritawase has never held a national Cha Norin registration; myjapanesegreentea.com's
cultivar list places it explicitly among Japan's unregistered tea cultivars.

### Sugiyama Hikosaburo's second island

The 1927 visit is worth placing in context. Sugiyama Hikosaburo spent most of his working life in
Shizuoka, where — beyond Yabukita itself — he went on to select the individual trees that became
Koyanishi (Cha Norin No. 8), Rokuro (Cha Norin No. 9) and Yaeho (Cha Norin No. 17), all from his
own or nearby Shizuoka gardens and all registered decades after his death in 1941. Kuritawase is
not related to any of these by descent — it is a wholly separate Shizuoka-zairai seedling, sown
and selected a thousand kilometres away on Tanegashima — but it shares the same breeder's eye at
its founding moment: the one occasion sourced in which Sugiyama is recorded travelling outside
Shizuoka to guide a selection was this 1927 trip to Kagoshima.

### Later spread and descendants

Kuritawase's most visible legacy runs through its own mutations and crosses rather than through
further national registration. Decades after the 1931 planting at the prefectural station,
Matsushita Eiichi — a Tanegashima grower whose family had also migrated from Shizuoka in 1909 —
noticed and propagated a branch mutation on a Kuritawase bush at Furuta that became Shoju,
registered under the Plant Variety Protection Act in 1996. Independently, breeders at the Shizuoka
Tea Experiment Station crossed Okuhikari with Kuritawase in 1989; the resulting seedling was
selected in 1997, carried the strain name 89-2-7 through clonal and regional-adaptability trials
into the 2010s, and was registered and adopted as a Shizuoka Prefecture recommended cultivar in
2015 under the name Shizukaori. More recently, local reporting shows the cultivar reaching beyond
Tanegashima: Hachimanju Chaen, a grower on neighbouring Yakushima, describes Kuritawase — which it
calls "a rare cultivar said to originate on Tanegashima" — as the first of its seven cultivars
picked each season, opening its shipping year with it every April.

## Characteristics

Kuritawase's defining trait is speed. myjapanesegreentea.com states it can be harvested at least
10 days before Yabukita; a controlled 1972 trial at Makurazaki recorded its optimum plucking date
as 13 April against Yabukita's 26 April in the same trial — 13 days earlier, and the earliest of
every cultivar tested that year, ahead of Makinoharawase, Hatsumidori, Yutakamidori, Yaeho and
Surugawase in that order. That earliness comes at a cost: myjapanesegreentea.com notes Kuritawase
is weak against cold weather, and a 1986 research-journal trial recorded a Kuritawase field
suffering total ("V"-grade, 100%) frost damage at the 1.3-leaf stage during a 1985 frost event — an
occupational hazard of budding before winter has fully released its grip. A related 2001 study
found that in unusually warm autumn–winters, young Kuritawase fields can re-sprout from winter
buds altogether, which risks weakening the following spring's first crop if not managed with
timed re-skiffing. Against this, Kuritawase is comparatively unbothered by two other pressures:
myjapanesegreentea.com describes it as somewhat resistant to anthracnose, and a 2002 laboratory
comparison of eight Japanese cultivars found it supported the fewest tea rust mite eggs of any
cultivar tested — evidence of below-average palatability to the mite. Yield at harvest is
described as low despite good cup quality; no kg/10a figure was located in the sources consulted.

The cup is distinctive for an early cultivar. myjapanesegreentea.com describes a yellow liquor
with a milky aroma and low astringency when made into sencha — a trait that resurfaces even more
strongly in Shoju, its own bud sport, whose milky, vanilla-like sweetness is one of the more
memorable descriptions in this whole collection. A Yakushima grower's regular-steamed batch is
described as pale in colour but firm and aromatic in the cup, made simply to let the natural
flavour show. Shinkoju, a single-cultivar tea specialist, sells Kuritawase across three processing
styles with tasting notes that keep returning to the same register: the regular sencha is likened
to potage soup, the deep-steamed (fukamushi) version to clam chowder with a warming aroma and a
darker green liquor, and a black-tea processing to sweet potato and milk soup, each rated low to
moderate on astringency and moderate on body. Whatever the exact chemistry behind it, a soup-like,
milky character runs through Kuritawase across sencha, fukamushi and black tea alike, and is
inherited wholesale by its most famous descendant.
