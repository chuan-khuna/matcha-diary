# Oku-no-yama

Oku-no-yama is a Kyoto tea cultivar registered under Japan's Plant Variety Protection Act in
2002, propagated by cuttings from a single, very old zairai (landrace, seed-grown) tea tree in
the Okunoyama garden in Uji. It is not a bred cultivar in the usual sense of a research
station's controlled cross: it is one plant, fixed by cloning, chosen from a population of
zairai trees that had never been catalogued at all. Against Yabukita it is a later-budding
plant with an elliptical mature leaf and, per its own registration record, more numerous
shoots at picking than the Uji standard Asahi - officially documented traits used to establish
that it is legally distinct from both. It is processed almost entirely as tencha and milled to
matcha, with a small gyokuro line also produced, and it exists nowhere outside the one garden
it is named for.

## Lineage

- **Uji zairai (landrace) seedling, Okunoyama garden** - an unnamed, seed-grown individual
  from the garden's standing population of native tea trees, propagated onward by cuttings
  from a single mother plant that Horii Shichimeien states is more than 400 years old.

Oku-no-yama has no controlled cross behind it and, strictly, no named parent: MAFF's own
registration record describes it as bred "by selection from among Uji zairai" (宇治在来種の中
から選抜をして育成), the same wording used for its sibling Narino. The two were registered on
the same day under sequential numbers - Narino as registered variety No. 10751 (application
No. 12426), Oku-no-yama as No. 10752 (application No. 12427) - both filed by Horii Nobuo on 13
March 2000, which is about as close to documentary proof of a shared origin as a pair of
zairai selections is likely to get. They are not clones of each other, though: Horii
Shichimeien's own account describes a single 20-year screening of the garden's zairai
population (1980-2000) that yielded two distinct plants worth keeping, of which Oku-no-yama
was judged the deeper green and more gyokuro-like, and Narino the higher in theanine. No
cultivar bred forward from Oku-no-yama is documented in any source consulted for this record.

## History

### The garden behind the name

Okunoyama is one of the historic Uji Seven Tea Gardens (宇治七茗園 / 七名園), the plantings
tradition credits to Ōuchi Yoshihiro, acting on the order of the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, and
whose existence is first documented in the 1564 text *Bunrui Sōjinmoku*. Horii Shichimeien's own
company name - 堀井七茗園, "Horii of the Seven Tea Gardens" - refers directly to this tradition. A
2019 radiocarbon-dating study of the Okunoyama garden's soil profile (Inoue et al., *Japanese
Journal of Soil Science and Plant Nutrition*) found phytolith and carbon evidence for shaded
cultivation (覆下栽培) there from at least the early fifteenth century - roughly 1400-1440 by the
paper's calibrated dates - about 150 years earlier than the practice had previously been
documented at Uji, and independently consistent with Horii Shichimeien's own claim that the
garden has been under continuous cultivation for close to 600 years. The paper describes
Okunoyama as the sole one of the historic seven gardens still in production today. Horii
Shichimeien states it took over the garden in the middle of the Meiji era (roughly the 1880s-90s).

### Selection and registration

Between 1980 and 2000, Horii Nobuo (堀井信夫), the family's fifth-generation head, worked through
the garden's zairai population looking for individual plants worth preserving as named
cultivars, rather than left as an anonymous mixed stand. Two were chosen: Narino and Oku-no-yama.
Of the latter, Horii is quoted as saying "there are no leaves this deep green - it's dyed just
like natural gyokuro," and MAFF's registration record credits that depth of colour as the basis
for the plant's legal distinctness. Only one mother tree - reportedly over 400 years old - stands
in the garden today; every Oku-no-yama plant is a cutting-propagated descendant of it.

An application for registration under the Plant Variety Protection Act (種苗法) was filed on 13
March 2000 (application No. 12427) and published on 20 October 2000. The variety was formally
registered on 14 November 2002 as registered variety No. 10752, with Horii Nobuo named as both
applicant and breeder, his address on file as 84 Uji-Myōraku, Uji City, Kyoto (611-0021). This is
a Plant Variety Protection Act registration, not a MAFF Cha Norin (茶農林) number, and not a
Kyoto prefectural recommended cultivar: neither the Kyoto Tea Trade Association's cultivar page
nor the prefectural tea research institute's own list Oku-no-yama among Kyoto's recommended
cultivars, which currently number just four (Hoshun, Ujimidori, Kyomidori and Asahi). The
registration record also states a 25-year statutory term for the breeder's right, but records
that right as having lapsed on 15 November 2011 - nine years in, not twenty-five. No source found
explains the discrepancy; a lapse of this kind more commonly reflects non-renewal of an annual
maintenance fee than the full term running its course, but that reason is not stated in the
record and should not be assumed.

### Today

Horii Shichimeien processes the whole of each year's Oku-no-yama harvest as tencha and mills it
into matcha under the name "Okunoyama". A separate gyokuro line is also produced, rolled to order
by a tea-rolling specialist in Uji-Shirakawa; it won first prize in the gyokuro category at the
2019 (Reiwa 1) Uji City Tea Competition. Both remain single-garden, low-volume products sold
directly by Horii Shichimeien rather than cultivated more widely.

## Characteristics

Oku-no-yama is a late cultivar. Its own registration record states plainly that both budding
(萌芽期) and picking (摘採期) run later than Yabukita's, and later again than Asahi's - the two
comparison cultivars MAFF used to establish its legal distinctness - though no source found
quantifies the gap in days. A separate cultivar reference (日本茶備忘録) independently classifies
it as 晩生 (late-budding) and a gyokuro cultivar, consistent with the registration record.

The registration's plant description gives a fuller picture than is normal for so obscure a
cultivar. Growth habit is slightly upright with strong vigour and a large stool spread; leaf
layer thickness is medium. At picking time the shoots are numerous and fairly uniform in
development. New leaves are somewhat elliptical, medium in size and thickness, deep green, and
notably glossy; mature leaves are elliptical, deep green, with a heavily wrinkled surface and
somewhat wavy margins. Branching is somewhat frequent, with somewhat short internodes and a
fairly steep leaf-attachment angle. Against Yabukita it is distinguished chiefly by that
elliptical mature-leaf shape and its later budding; against Asahi, by more numerous shoots at
picking time and, again, later budding.

The cup follows the leaf: a deep, even green when milled, which Horii Shichimeien frames
explicitly against gyokuro rather than sencha or ordinary tencha - "dyed just like natural
gyokuro," in Horii Nobuo's own description. The registration record itself rates finished-product
appearance, aroma, liquor colour and flavour all above average, and Horii's own tasting notes
describe a mild, unforced umami rather than an assertive one. The gyokuro version is described as
elegant, with a pronounced 覆い香 (ooika, the "covering aroma" characteristic of well-shaded tea).

As with Narino, there is no multi-region agronomic trial data for Oku-no-yama - no published
yield in kg/10a, no disease-resistance rating - because it has never been grown outside the one
garden it was selected in. What does exist, unusually for a cultivar this obscure, is the
detailed morphological description filed with its own Plant Variety Protection Act registration,
which is the basis for most of the characteristics recorded above.
