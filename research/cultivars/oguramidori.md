# Ogura-midori

Ogura-midori is a tencha cultivar that came out of Kyoto Prefecture's Uji landrace selection
programme in the early 1950s and is still on the prefecture's recommended list today. Unlike
Ujihikari and Gokou, which the Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research Institute selected itself,
Ogura-midori came in from outside: it is one of four private selections — with Samidori, Asahi and
Komakage — that the institute took into its trials, raised and named. It is far less visible at
retail than those siblings, and almost nothing has been published about where it came from, but
Kyoto still counts it among the ten 宇治種 and still pays growers to plant it.

## Lineage

- **Kyoto/Uji indigenous tea tree (zairai)** — an individual selected from seed-grown native
  stock, not a controlled cross; the institute's chronology records it only as a 民間選抜, a
  private breeder's selection, without naming the grower or the stand, unlike Asahi and Komakage
  (Hirano Jinnojō) and Samidori (Koyama Masajirō), whose selecting growers are named in Kyoto
  Prefecture's own records.

Because it is a landrace selection rather than a cross, Ogura-midori has no recorded pollen
parent. It has no documented descendants, and no cultivar in this collection is recorded as bred
from it.

## History

The Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research Institute began selecting from zairai stock in 1939, and from
that same year also admitted selections made by private breeders into its cultivar trials. In 1953
it raised and named the results: its own selections Asagiri, Kyō-midori, Gokou and Ujihikari, and
the private selections Samidori, Ogura-midori, Asahi and Komakage. The following year, 1954, the
prefecture fixed ten cultivars as 京都府茶奨励品種, its recommended tea cultivars — a list
Ogura-midori is still on, with a full row of published cultivation characteristics beside the
better-known Uji names.

Nor is that list only a formality. Kyoto now groups its distinctive cultivars as the 宇治種 —
Uji-midori, Kyō-midori, Samidori, Asahi, Ujihikari, Ogura-midori, Gokou, Komakage, Hōshun and
Tenmyō — which together made up 22% of the prefecture's tea plantings by 2023, against 16% in
2008, and since 2021 the prefecture has run a replanting subsidy (宇治種改植加速化支援事業) aimed
at moving growers off Yabukita and onto them. O-CHA NET names Asahi and Ogura-midori together as
the representative cultivars grown for tencha.

The name points to where the selection is tied: Ogura (小倉) is a district in the western part of
Uji City, formerly the separate village of Ogura, merged into Uji in 1951. It sat on the shore of
Ogura-ike (巨椋池), a large lake drained for farmland by 1941, and has been a tea-producing part of
the Uji district since at least the Edo period — Marukyū Koyamaen, one of Uji's principal matcha
producers, was founded there in the Genroku era (1688–1704) and still operates its head office and
factory at Ogura-chō, Terauchi 86. Whether Ogura-midori was selected from a planting in Ogura
itself, or simply follows the naming pattern of siblings such as Kyō-midori and Uji-midori (place
name plus -midori), is not stated in any source found.

What is thin is the origin story rather than the cultivar. No breeding paper, strain number or
selection site for Ogura-midori was located in Kyoto Prefecture's publications, the national MAFF
variety-registration systems, or the Japanese cultivar literature: the institute's chronology gives
it a single line, and where its siblings carry strain numbers on the record — 京研166号 for Gokou,
京研170号 for Ujihikari, 平野11号 for Asahi, 小山69号 for Samidori — Ogura-midori has no published
counterpart.

## Characteristics

Kyoto Prefecture's cultivation-characteristics table for its recommended cultivars gives the
picture. Ogura-midori buds mid-season (中生), on par with Yabukita; its bush habit, spread, rooting
from cuttings, early growth and yield are all rated 中 — medium — straight across, and its new
leaves under cover are oval, medium in size, green, of medium gloss and soft. It carries medium
resistance to anthracnose (炭疽病), while the ring-spot (輪斑病) cell is left blank where Gokou and
Komakage beside it are both rated 強. It is rated 適, suitable, for the cold parts of the
prefecture — a note the same table withholds from Uji-midori and Ujihikari, both of which it warns
away from cold districts. Its one ◎ is for tencha, and the table's verdict on the made tea is
a single phrase: てん茶として優良, "excellent as tencha."

Tencha, like gyokuro, is grown under cover in the final weeks before picking, which suppresses
catechin formation and concentrates theanine, producing the characteristic umami, low astringency
and deep green colour of Uji-style shaded tea. Kyoto rates several of its cultivars ◎ for gyokuro
as well — Gokou and Komakage among them — but Ogura-midori is not one of them: on the prefecture's
own reckoning it is a tencha cultivar and nothing else.
