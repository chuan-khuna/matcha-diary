# Ogura-midori

Ogura-midori is one of a small, largely forgotten batch of tea trees selected from Kyoto and Uji
indigenous (zairai) stock in 1954 by the Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research Institute, earmarked for
gyokuro and tencha production. That same year and programme also produced Asahi, Gokō,
Uji-hikari and Samidori — cultivars that went on to define modern Uji tencha and gyokuro — but
Ogura-midori did not follow them into commercial release. It survives today mainly as a name in
cultivar registries rather than as a documented, cultivated tea.

## Lineage

- **Kyoto/Uji indigenous tea tree (zairai)** — an individual selected from seed-grown native
  stock, not a controlled cross; no source records which stand it was taken from or who selected
  it, unlike Asahi (Hirano Jinnojō) and Samidori (Koyama Masajirō), whose selecting growers are
  named in Kyoto Prefecture's own records.

Because it is a landrace selection rather than a cross, Ogura-midori has no recorded pollen
parent. It has no documented descendants, and no cultivar in this collection is recorded as bred
from it.

## History

In 1954, the Kyoto Prefectural Tea Research Institute selected a group of individual trees out of
Kyoto and Uji zairai plantings for gyokuro and tencha use. Six names from that effort are recorded
in the wider cultivar literature: Asahi, Gokō, Uji-hikari, Samidori, Komakage and Ogura-midori.
Four of them — Asahi and Samidori (tencha), Gokō and, later, Uji-midori (gyokuro) — were developed
further, promoted by the prefecture, and remain the standard hand-picked cultivars behind
high-grade Uji tencha and gyokuro to this day. Komakage and Ogura-midori were not: neither appears
on Kyoto Prefecture's current cultivar pages, neither has a MAFF Cha Norin registration number or
a Plant Variety Protection Act registration, and neither turns up in the vendor, research-station
or prefectural literature searched for this record beyond a bare listing.

The name points to where the selection is tied: Ogura (小倉) is a district in the western part of
Uji City, formerly the separate village of Ogura, merged into Uji in 1951. It sat on the shore of
Ogura-ike (巨椋池), a large lake drained for farmland by 1941, and has been a tea-producing part of
the Uji district since at least the Edo period — Marukyū Koyamaen, one of Uji's principal matcha
producers, was founded there in the Genroku era (1688–1704) and still operates its head office and
factory at Ogura-chō, Terauchi 86. Whether Ogura-midori was selected from a planting in Ogura
itself, or simply follows the naming pattern of siblings such as Kyō-midori and Uji-midori (place
name plus -midori), is not stated in any source found.

No trial data, characteristics bulletin, or registration file for Ogura-midori was located despite
searching Kyoto Prefecture's tea research pages, the national MAFF variety-registration systems,
and both English- and Japanese-language cultivar literature. The record here is limited to what
the master cultivar registries state.

## Characteristics

Budding time, yield, disease resistance and cup characteristics are not documented in any source
found for this record, and none is invented here. What can be said is only what the registries
record: it was selected for gyokuro and tencha, the shaded tea categories, which as a class are
grown under cover in the final weeks before picking to suppress catechin formation and concentrate
theanine, producing the characteristic umami, low astringency and deep green colour of Uji-style
gyokuro and tencha. Whether Ogura-midori itself achieved this well enough to be worth growing —
and if so, why it was not carried forward the way Asahi, Samidori, Uji-hikari and Gokō were — is
not recorded in the sources available. Its absence from every current cultivar list, alongside its
sibling Komakage, is itself the most informative fact this research turned up: not every 1954
Kyoto selection proved out, and the ones that did not have left almost no trace.
